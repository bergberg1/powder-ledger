# Crest-Level Wind Decides Which Mountain Wins

**Bottom line:** For a seven-day snowfall outlook at the ski areas within roughly five hours of Denver and across Utah, the best stack as of mid-October 2026 is the ECMWF IFS ensemble for the day 3-10 storm track, the 3-km HRRR (joined on October 14, 2026 by the RRFS/REFS that retire the NAM, HREF and HiresW) plus the National Blend of Models v5.0 for terrain detail inside 48 hours, and human forecasters to correct both, because every model, global or high-resolution, under-forecasts liquid at the crests by roughly a quarter to a third (GFS -33% and HRRR -29% at Alta over four winters; 25-65% low at Colorado SNOTEL sites). The reasoning behind a forecast lives in free text: the four NWS Area Forecast Discussions (Boulder, Grand Junction, Pueblo, Salt Lake City), OpenSnow's Colorado and Utah Daily Snow (Joel Gratz, Evan Thayer), Jim Steenburgh's Wasatch Weather Weenies, the CAIC's twice-daily mountain forecasts and in-house WRF, BoulderCAST and Chris Tomer; paid tiers (OpenSnow Base $49.99/yr, Premium $99.99/yr; WeatherBELL $29.99/mo) buy data depth, not narrative. Machine-readable feeds are free and browser-callable from Open-Meteo (GFS, HRRR, NBM, ECMWF IFS/AIFS, ICON, GEM, UKMO, plus per-member GEFS and IFS ensembles, with 700-hPa temperature, wind and humidity), api.weather.gov (raw gridpoint snowfall and AFD text), the NRCS AWDB SNOTEL API, WPC's WSSI ArcGIS services and avalanche.org. To understand why one mountain out-snows another in a given storm, a skier checks five things in order: where the upper low sits (Pacific trough, cutoff/Four Corners/Albuquerque low, split flow, atmospheric river), the 700-mb wind direction against each range's windward face (northwest flow favors the Cottonwoods, Steamboat and the I-70 corridor; southwest flow favors Wolf Creek, the San Juans, Snowbasin, Sundance and the Wasatch Back; easterly upslope favors Eldora and the Front Range), moisture depth and instability, crest temperature and snow level, and the snow-to-liquid ratio (Alta median 13.3:1, Front Range 12-18:1, range 3.6-35.7), then uses ensemble spread and model-to-model agreement as the confidence gauge. The current season opens under a strong-to-very-strong El Niño (Niño-3.4 at +2.1°C on October 8, 2026, >83% odds of persisting through January-March 2027) that tilts southern Colorado and southern Utah wet, leaves northern/central Colorado west of the Divide without a signal, and is mixed for the Wasatch; the October 13-20 week brings the remnants of former Hurricane Rachel plus a cold Great Basin trough, with 4-10 inches in the upper Cottonwoods, up to two feet in the western Uintas, one to two feet in the San Juans, a few inches above 10,000 feet on the Front Range, a mild dry weekend October 17-18, and a possible repeat around October 21-23. Every value in this report is tagged by whether the underlying notes verified it against a cited source; the research team could not fetch most primary pages directly (egress blocked), so quotations come from search snippets, example API URLs are composed from documentation but untested, and roughly half of the ski-area table (coordinates, drive times, several SNOTEL IDs) is flagged as unverified general knowledge that must be checked before publication.

## ECMWF for the pattern, HRRR and NBM for terrain, humans for the crest

The operational model lineup changed materially in 2026, and anyone comparing this winter's forecasts with last year's needs the new vocabulary. ECMWF implemented IFS Cycle 50r1 on May 12, 2026, jointly with AIFS Single v2.0 and AIFS ENS v2.0; the cycle brought "no changes to horizontal, vertical or temporal resolution," but **"the data stream previously known as HRES will become the new ENS control"**, so the deterministic Euro many forecasters quote is now formally the control of the 51-member, 9-km ensemble ([ECMWF 50r1/AIFS2 presentation](https://events.ecmwf.int/event/531/attachments/3520/5948/50r1_AIFS2_presentation.pdf); [ECMWF UEF2024](https://events.ecmwf.int/event/397/contributions/4524/attachments/2584/4445/UEF2024%20ECMWF%20progress%20and%20plans%20-%20Andy%20Brown%20and%20Florian%20Pappenberger.pdf)). The AIFS machine-learning models run at about 0.25° (~28-32 km) and are useful for synoptic track and timing at days 5-10, but ECMWF's own known-issues page warns that "at about 28 km resolution, AIFS can't resolve small extremes such as orographic rainfall or temperature extremes in valleys and on mountain tops... AIFS makes these problems worse because of the smoothing," and the ECMWF Newsletter notes "unrealistic cold biases at high-altitude sites" in AI output ([ECMWF Known AIFS Issues](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/spaces/FCST/pages/496875172/Known+AIFS+Single+Forecasting+Issues); [ECMWF Newsletter 185](https://www.ecmwf.int/node/29518)). On the American side, the operational GFS is still v16 (13 km, FV3, implemented March 2021); NCEP proposed GFS v17 (9 km, fully coupled) for "October 2026," but the notes found no Service Change Notice with an effective date as of October 13, so treat v17 as pending and expect product-directory changes when it lands ([NWS PNS 26-29](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/pns26-29_Science_for_GFSv17.pdf); [EMC GFS documentation](https://emc.ncep.noaa.gov/emc/pages/numerical_forecast_systems/gfs/documentation.php)). The GEFS remains v12: 31 members at ~25 km, four runs daily, 16 days (35 days from the 00 UTC run), and critically **snowfall "is not directly forecast by the Global Ensemble Forecast System"**, so every GEFS snow plume you see is a post-processed ratio applied by the site that drew it ([EMC GEFS page](https://www.emc.ncep.noaa.gov/emc/pages/numerical_forecast_systems/gefs.php); [EMC GEFS FAQ](https://www.emc.ncep.noaa.gov/emc/pages/numerical_forecast_systems/gefs/faq.php)). The Canadian GDPS runs at 15 km to 10 days, the UKMO global at ~10 km to 7 days, and DWD's ICON at 13 km to 180 hours ([Government of Canada GDPS](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/c041e79a-914a-5a4e-a485-9cbc506195df); [LuckGrib UKMET](https://luckgrib.com/models/ukmet_global/); [DWD ICON](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/DACH2016/DACH2016-69.pdf)).

The biggest short-range change is scheduled for the day after the research date. NWS SCN 26-48 (updated September 9, 2026) set the RRFS/REFS implementation for **October 14, 2026**, after earlier targets of August 31 and October 6; the 3-km FV3-based RRFS "will fully retire the North American Mesoscale (NAM)" and REFS "will fully retire the High-Resolution Ensemble Forecast (HREF) system," with NAM, SREF, HREF, HiresW and NAM MOS all retiring the same day and downstream changes effective November 3 ([NWS SCN 26-48 updated](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-048_Updated_RRFS_and_REFS_Implementation_aad.pdf); [NWS SCN 26-47](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-47_Retirement_of_NAM_SREF_HREF_HiresW_NAM_MOS.pdf); [NWS SCN 26-89](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-89_RRFS_Downstream.pdf)). RRFS runs 18-hour forecasts hourly and 84-hour forecasts at 00/06/12/18 UTC over North America, while the HRRR (3 km, 48 hours at 00/06/12/18 UTC, 18 hours otherwise) "will remain in operations" alongside it ([NOAA GSL RRFS](https://gsl.noaa.gov/rrfs); [CSU HRRR page](https://schumacher.atmos.colostate.edu/weather/hrrr.php)). Early verification is mixed: a January 2025 WPC Winter Weather Experiment slide found "at shorter lead times, HRRR slightly better than RRFS," and a Utah machine-learning snow-ratio model trained on RRFS inputs "still struggled to capture outlier event (20:1 SLRs observed)" ([WPC WWE Jan 21 2025](https://origin-east-www-wpc.woc.noaa.gov/hmt/hmt_webpages/seminars/wwe/2025/slides_20250121.pdf)). The notes could not confirm whether the October 14 cutover actually happened, so sites still labeled "NAM 3km" or "HREF" this week may be serving their last runs.

The National Blend of Models is the product that explains most official NWS snow numbers, because it is the first-guess grid that forecast offices edit. **NBM v5.0, implemented around April 30, 2026**, "moves away from the 1% equal-weighting of the 100 model inputs used in V4.3" toward expert weighting that favors high-resolution inputs, produces snow and ice accumulation, snow-to-liquid ratio, snow level, precipitation type and snow depth, and adds "new calibrated probabilities for snow amounts (e.g., Prob > 6")" ([NWS SCN 26-24](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-24_Updated_NBM_V5.0_aac.pdf); [NBM v5.0 overview](https://vlab.noaa.gov/documents/6609493/7858320/NBMv5.0+Overview+for+Evaluation.pdf); [Blend Winter v5.0 configuration](https://vlab.noaa.gov/documents/6609493/7858320/Blend_Winter_v5.0-Configuration_and_Technical_Details.pdf)). Its inputs include the HRRR (prioritized early), 30 GEFS members and 50 ECMWF ensemble members, and its snow ratio uses the Cobb method, which weights temperature, vertical velocity and humidity through the cloud layer ([Veals et al. 2025](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~u0813947/sharing/Veals_etal_2025_v1.5.pdf); [Cobb SLR in NBM, WPC 2024](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/hmt/hmt_webpages/seminars/wwe/2024/20240314_Cobb_SLR_methodology_application_NBM.pdf)). In Colorado, the CAIC runs its own nested WRF over the state four times daily, displayed on its site as "CAIC WRF HiRes" with hourly point forecasts, time-height cross-sections and wind roses for predetermined mountain points, which makes it the best free terrain-aware model source for Colorado resorts ([CAIC forecast models](https://avalanche.state.co.us/weather/forecast-models/about); [ISSW 2016 CAIC paper](https://arc.lib.montana.edu/snow-science/objects/ISSW16_O15.01.pdf)).

The reason human forecasters still beat raw model output in the mountains is a documented, systematic bias. Caron and Steenburgh's western-US verification found that "the GFSv14, GFSv15.0, and HRES all exhibit a wet bias at lower elevations and neutral or dry bias at upper elevations, reflecting insufficient terrain representation," and that GFSv15 "lags HRES" ([Caron & Steenburgh 2020](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~steenburgh/cstar/papers/WAF-S-19-00225.pdf)). At Alta-Collins across the 2019-20 to 2022-23 cool seasons, **"the 12-h QPFs produced by the GFS and HRRR underpredict the total LPE... by 33% and 29%, respectively"** ([Veals et al. 2025](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~u0813947/sharing/Veals_etal_2025_v1.5.pdf)), and a Colorado Rockies study found gridded products and forecasts including the HRRR "underestimate cold-season precipitation by 25%-65% relative to in situ and aircraft measurements" ([NOAA repository 69337](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/69337/noaa_69337_DS1.pdf)). The GEFS shows "widespread dry biases, which reflect the infrequent production of larger 24-h precipitation events" at western SNOTEL sites ([NOAA GSL publication](https://publications.gsl.noaa.gov/articles/1551)). The practical reading rule that follows: a global model's 13-25 km grid point is coarser than any single Wasatch or Colorado range, so read its QPF as "how much moisture and forcing the storm brings to the region," and when a 3-km model shows an inch of liquid at a favored crest, expect forecasters to nudge up. The practitioner consensus matches the literature: OpenSnow's Utah forecaster writes that "the Euro... has been more consistent" while "short-term high-resolution models... are often the most accurate" inside 48 hours, and Gratz uses experience "to decide which models to trust and how to adjust their output" ([OpenSnow Ski Utah post](https://opensnow.com/dailysnow/skiutah/post/15372); [Colorado Sun OpenSnow profile](https://coloradosun.com/2023/03/12/opensnow-snow-reports-joel-gratz/)). One caveat the notes flag honestly: no peer-reviewed study ranks ECMWF versus GFS versus HRRR versus NBM for Colorado resort snowfall specifically; SNOTEL-based liquid verification is the closest proxy, because snow verification in the mountains is itself unreliable (few gauges, blocked radar, variable ratios) ([UCAR wiki note](https://wiki.ucar.edu/x/uKGAEg)).

## AFDs and OpenSnow top a ranked directory of "explain-the-why" sources

The research team ranked sources by how well they explain reasoning, not just totals, and the ordering is defensible on structural grounds: the NWS Area Forecast Discussion is the only product that is free, updated at least twice daily plus intermediate issuances, written by the forecasters who own the official grids, and whose "primary purpose... is to provide the meteorological/scientific reasoning behind the forecast," including "how confident we are, what could go wrong, alternate scenarios" ([NWS Glossary](https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=area+forecast+discussion); [NWS Raleigh forecast process](https://www.weather.gov/rah/virtualtourfcstcreation); [NWS Raleigh AFD handout](https://www.weather.gov/media/rah/NCStateStudents/handout.area.forecast.discussions.pdf)). Four offices cover the territory: **Boulder (BOU)** for the Front Range, Summit County and Grand County; **Grand Junction (GJT)** for the western slope from Steamboat and Vail to Aspen, Crested Butte, Telluride and Silverton plus eastern Utah; **Pueblo (PUB)** for the Upper Arkansas (Ski Cooper, Monarch) and the east side of Wolf Creek Pass; and **Salt Lake City (SLC)** for Utah. The direct links, with `version=1` for the latest issuance and higher numbers for older ones, are [AFD BOU](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=NWS&issuedby=BOU&product=AFD&format=CI&version=1&glossary=0), [AFD GJT](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=gjt&product=afd&issuedby=gjt), [AFD PUB](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=PUB&issuedby=PUB&product=AFD&format=CI&version=1&glossary=1) and [AFD SLC](https://forecast.weather.gov/product.php?site=NWS&issuedby=slc&product=AFD&format=CI&version=1&glossary=1). Read the `.LONG TERM` section and, in Central Region offices like BOU/GJT/PUB, the mandatory `.KEY MESSAGES` block, because that is where multi-day storm-track and model-spread reasoning is stated ([NWS Central Region supplement](https://www.weather.gov/media/directives/010_pdfs/pd01005003c102023curr.pdf)). The SLC office also publishes a daily mountain-weather page for the Cottonwood and Provo canyons organized by Utah Avalanche Center zone ([NWS SLC Avalanche Weather](https://www.weather.gov/slc/AvalancheWeather)).

OpenSnow is the best single consumer source because its state Daily Snow posts are the only daily, human-written, state-specific narratives that reference storm track, snow levels and model uncertainty in plain language, and the text is free. Joel Gratz, OpenSnow's founding meteorologist, writes Colorado; Evan Thayer, who started Wasatch Snow Forecast in 2010, writes Utah; Alan Smith writes the US+Canada roundup ([OpenSnow Colorado](https://opensnow.com/dailysnow/colorado); [OpenSnow Utah](https://opensnow.com/dailysnow/utah); [Keystone Science School profile](https://www.keystonescienceschool.org/donate/events/speaker-series-joel-gratz-from-opensnow/)). Since November 2025 the paid tiers are **Base at $49.99/yr and Premium at $99.99/yr** (group plans $89.99 and $179.99 for four people), which unlock the PEAKS AI-model 15-day forecasts, Forecast Anywhere and precipitation-type maps rather than any additional narrative; new accounts get a Premium preview with no card required ([OpenSnow Subscription](https://opensnow.com/subscription); [OpenSnow Free Preview](https://support.opensnow.com/subscription/free-preview)). PEAKS, launched December 2025, "adapts standard global weather models through machine learning," runs on a 3-km grid, and OpenSnow claims (its own figure) "up to 50 percent more accurate forecasts in mountainous terrain" ([South Tahoe Now](https://southtahoenow.com/12/14/2025/opensnow-launches-peaks-a-groundbreaking-ai-forecast-model-for-mountain-weather); [OpenSnow AI FAQ](https://support.opensnow.com/faq/opensnow-ai)). Its model-disagreement language is typically framed as confidence in track ("confidence in the storm track is low") plus a range of totals rather than naming GFS versus ECMWF in the summaries that surfaced ([OpenSnow Daily Snows](https://opensnow.com/dailysnow)).

## How to read a storm: five checks from 500 mb to snow ratio

Joel Gratz's framing is the right starting point: **"If a big storm is coming, the computer models will all say 'snow.' But you need to know topographically what that means,"** and "in big mountains here in Colorado, two-thirds of the time, the wind direction is a really big deal," though storm energy can override an unfavorable direction ([Elevation Outdoors](https://elevationoutdoors.com/?p=44186); [Colorado Sun 2023](https://coloradosun.com/2023/03/12/colorado-ski-resorts-best-snow-conditions/)). The sequence below is assembled from how the AFDs, OpenSnow, BoulderCAST, Steenburgh and the NWS case studies actually reason.

**Check 1, the storm track and its pattern name (500-mb map).** Where the upper low sits relative to the ranges sets the crest-level wind for everything downstream. An open Pacific trough sweeping west-to-east gives southwest flow ahead of it and northwest flow behind it, so the Wasatch and San Juans get the warm front side and the Cottonwoods, Steamboat and I-70 corridor get the colder post-frontal side; OpenSnow notes the I-70 corridor and Winter Park "sneak up higher totals" when winds shift northwest behind a system ([OpenSnow I-70 Daily Snow](https://opensnow.com/dailysnow/i70/post/7727)). A cutoff low is one that "breaks off from the main flow," and because cutoffs "move slowly or not at all because they are isolated from the west to east flow of the jet stream," they produce multi-day events ([KOAA: Albuquerque low](https://koaa.com/weather/weather-science/what-an-albuquerque-low-is-and-how-its-creating-near-white-out-conditions); [CSU CMMAP](https://hogback.atmos.colostate.edu/cmmap/learn/weather/regional5.html)). The "Albuquerque low" is "a strong low that tracks northeast near Albuquerque and then through southeastern Colorado," giving "long-duration events that produce more snow in Southern Colorado than in northern areas like Denver"; the "Four Corners low" is the forecaster shorthand for the same southwest-flow, southern-track setup that maximizes Wolf Creek, the San Juans and Sangre de Cristos, with WPC's March 2024 case showing a closed low just west of the Four Corners delivering 50-80% odds of 8-12 inches and locally two feet ([KOAA](https://koaa.com/weather/weather-science/what-an-albuquerque-low-is-and-how-its-creating-near-white-out-conditions); [WPC storm summary 2024](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/storm_summaries/2024/storm10/stormsum_3.html)). The Front Range wrinkle is that a Four Corners low only helps Denver and the foothills if energy transfers to a new surface low over southeastern Colorado, which turns the low-level wind east-northeast "producing upslope enhancement into the Front Range and Palmer Divide"; otherwise, as BoulderCAST explains, the track "usually gives Boulder only a brief shot of upslope before the winds quickly switch to northwesterly (downslope)," and "on the north side of the low, upslope will be prevalent and deep. However, south of the low, dreaded downslope will be a factor" ([WPC QPF discussion Mar 2021](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/archives/discussions/qpfhsd/KWBCQPFHSD_20210321_084741); [BoulderCAST boom scenario](https://bouldercast.com/the-boom-scenario-is-looking-slightly-more-likely-for-tonight-bumping-up-snow-totals/); [BoulderCAST p=27195](https://bouldercast.com/?p=27195)). Split flow sends energy both north and south of Colorado with sharp gradients, as in forecasts where "most of the snow will fall along and south of Interstate 70" ([CBS Colorado](https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/latest-forecast-more-wind-in-store-today-ahead-of-next-storm/)). An atmospheric river into Utah means prolonged southwest flow, high snow levels (a December 2024 AR pushed them to ~7,500 feet with base-area rain) and dense snow, typically finishing with a cold front after which "winds shift again to a northwest flow. This upslope setup will allow mountain snow to crank" ([TownLift Dec 2024](https://townlift.com/2024/12/more-snow-on-the-way-1-2-feet-expected-in-utah-as-atmospheric-river-moisture-arrives); [KSL AR](https://www.ksl.com/article/news/outdoors/winter-storm-warnings-issued-across-utah-ahead-of-incoming-atmospheric-river/51251542)). A useful asymmetry: a track 100 miles north or south matters far more for the Front Range (upslope versus downslope) than for Wolf Creek, which does well across most southwest-component tracks.

**Check 2, the 700-mb wind direction against each range.** The 700-mb level (~10,000 feet, roughly crest height) is the reference level forecasters use, and NWS Grand Junction states the principle plainly: "snowfall distributions in the western Colorado mountains are largely dependent on the aspect of the topography and the wind direction" ([NWS GJT Grand Mesa study](https://www.weather.gov/media/gjt/Research/Heavy_Snow_Grand_Mesa_2006.pdf)). Speed matters as much as direction; BoulderCAST notes an east wind of 20 mph versus 10 mph "is twice as efficient of upslope" ([BoulderCAST](https://bouldercast.com/?p=2810)). The Great Salt Lake adds a Utah-only term. NWS Salt Lake City's criteria, from Steenburgh et al. (2000), are a **lake-minus-700-mb temperature difference of at least 16°C**, no capping inversion below 700 mb, limited (<60°) directional shear in the 800-600-mb steering layer, and low-level convergence over the lake; University of Utah work found events with differences as low as 12.4°C in midwinter and recommends a seasonal threshold ([NWS WR TA-lite 03-16](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/talite/talite0316.pdf); [Krueger et al. WAF](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~krueger/5220/waf-d-12-00016.1.pdf)). Lake effect forms with "fairly cold westerly, northwesterly, or northerly storms" and hits hardest south and east of the lake in the Cottonwoods, but it averages only about 5% of Cottonwood cool-season precipitation (0 to 10-12% by year; 5.1% of SWE at Snowbird over 12 years), mostly in October-November, so it is a late-storm bonus rather than the main event ([Ski Utah lake effect](https://www.skiutah.com/blog/what-is-a-lake-effect-storm-utah-s); [ScienceDaily 2013](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/02/130219090547.htm); [FirstTracks quoting Steenburgh](https://firsttracksonline.com/boards/threads/altabird-ut-4-14-15-12.10193/page-2)). Steenburgh keeps a live diagnostics page for it ([GSL lake diagnostics](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~u0028395/lake_diagnostics/)).

**Check 3, moisture depth and instability.** For southwest-flow and atmospheric-river events, precipitable water and integrated vapor transport scale the outcome, and the IPEX Wasatch case measured 1-2 m/s of windward ascent "where precipitation was roughly double that observed in the lowlands upstream," with Alta-to-Salt Lake precipitation ratios of ~180-278% across storm stages ([Stony Brook IPEX](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/the-kinematic-structure-of-a-wasatch-mountain-winter-storm-during/); [U. Utah orographic lecture](https://home.chpc.utah.edu/~u0035056/old_content/oldclass/3000/Lecture.13.1.2006.OrographicPrecip.pdf)). For northwest-flow Cottonwood events the opposite holds: IVT "is a weaker guide to LCC extremes than it is in many other ranges," and "during post-cold-frontal northwesterly flow, highly localized and intense snowfall can occur despite low IVT," which is why Alta's biggest events are often low-moisture, high-efficiency storms, such as the northwest pulse that "dropped almost two feet of 6% density snow in less than 24 hours" ([Wasserstein & Steenburgh](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10655280); [Alta blog](https://www.alta.com/blog/60-inches-in-100-hours)). On a model, look for 700-mb relative humidity of 80-90% (saturation at crest level), wind of 20-30 knots or more into the windward face, and steep post-frontal lapse rates, which produce the showery "overachieving" snow that models with a dry bias miss.

**Check 4, crest temperature and snow level.** NWS Salt Lake City's Alta technique uses 700 mb as the reference level with a ~3.3°C per 1,000 feet lapse rate down to the ski area ([NWS WR TA 00-11](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TAs/ta0011.pdf)); a Sierra-calibrated rule puts the snow level around 8,000 feet when the 700-mb temperature is 0°C in moderate-to-heavy precipitation, roughly 500 feet lower per degree colder, but the notes flag that this lookup is not calibrated for Colorado or Utah ([Shasta Avalanche Center](https://www.shastaavalanche.org/node/4864)). Note also that 700 mb sits below the highest Colorado terrain, so "there are no values for wind, temperature, and relative humidity in these locations" on some grids ([NOAA](https://noaa.gov/node/10507)). In warm events, as with the October 10-12, 2026 Rachel remnants that rained above 9,000 feet, the question becomes how much precipitation falls after the snow level drops, which is why 24-hour advisory totals and 5-day forecaster sums can differ by a factor of three for the same storm.

**Check 5, the snow-to-liquid ratio.** Snowfall is liquid times ratio, and the ratio varies more than most skiers assume. Operational methods are Kuchera (maximum column temperature below 500 hPa, "never formally published" but widely used; "can overstate totals, particularly more than 72 hours out"), Roebber (a neural network), Cobb (temperature, vertical velocity and humidity weighted through the cloud; used in NBM since v4.1), and in Utah the Alcott-Steenburgh regression, in which near-crest 650-hPa temperature (R = -0.62) and wind speed (R = -0.39) explain 68% of ratio variance at Alta, which the NWS adopted across Utah ([NOAA repository 55878](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/55878/noaa_55878_DS1.pdf); [Pivotal snowfall guide](https://home.pivotalweather.com/guides/snowfall); [Veals et al. 2025](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~u0813947/sharing/Veals_etal_2025_v1.5.pdf); [NWS WR TA 13-03](https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TAs/TA1303.pdf)). The climatology to anchor on: **Alta median 13.3:1 with a range of 3.6 to 35.7**, mean water content 8.4%; Front Range ratios "generally between 12:1 and 18:1," with cold upslope events pushing past 17:1 and warm March storms near 10:1; and a 30-year national climatology showing "a mean SLR value of 13 appears more appropriate for much of the country rather than the often-assumed value of 10" ([Steenburgh VLab 2024](https://vlab.noaa.gov/documents/10157/137122/Steenburgh-VLabForum-11-20-2024.pdf); [KUTV](https://kutv.com/news/local/does-utah-really-have-the-39greatest-snow-on-earth39); [BoulderCAST SLR 101](https://bouldercast.com/forecasting-101-what-are-snow-to-liquid-ratios-and-why-do-we-care/); [Baxter 2005](https://www.eas.slu.edu/CIPS/PUBLICATIONS/Baxter2005.pdf)). The physics: the dendritic growth zone of -12 to -18°C with at least 75-80% humidity and strong lift yields the highest ratios; "if the omega maximum is below the DGZ... shallow lift and low snow-liquid ratios. If it is above the DGZ, the snow tends to be fine and columnar or platy, again with low ratios" ([NWS P-Type training](https://www.weather.gov/media/zhu/ZHU_Training_Page/winter_stuff/Winter_Stuff/Forecasting_P_Type.pdf); [Cadima SNEWC 2014](https://bluehill.org/wp-content/uploads/snewc/2014/Cadima_Snowstorms_SNEWC_2014.pdf)). Steamboat's 45-year Storm Peak Lab record says its biggest "champagne" days need temperatures between -3°F and 10°F, northwest tracks and winds up to ~20 mph ([SnowBrains Storm Peak Lab](https://snowbrains.com/scientists-uncover-the-ideal-conditions-causing-big-champagne-powder-days-at-steamboat-co-speedy-snowflakes/)). A non-meteorologist can apply this in three steps: find the model 700-mb (crest) temperature, where about -5°C with strong wind means dense 8-10:1 "cement" and -10 to -15°C with light wind means 15-20:1 "champagne"; check whether the strongest lift on a model sounding sits inside the -12 to -18°C band; multiply liquid by the chosen ratio. The same inch of liquid gives 8 inches of wet snow or 20 of blower. A subtle trap the Utah research exposed: at Alta, "QSFs derived from operational SLR methods produce higher critical success indices since they exhibit positive SLR biases that offset negative QPF biases," so raw NBM snow totals in the Cottonwoods often look right for the wrong reasons, and the big busts come when both biases point the same way, as in cold northwest-flow events where liquid is underdone and the real ratio is 20:1 or more ([Veals et al. 2025](https://www.inscc.utah.edu/~u0813947/sharing/Veals_etal_2025_v1.5.pdf)). Open-Meteo's `snowfall` field hard-codes a 7:1 ratio for every model except NBM, so it will systematically under-forecast depth in these ranges; recover the liquid and apply your own ratio (see the API section).

**Check 6, model agreement and ensemble spread as the confidence gauge.** The standard rule is to "use the ensemble mean as the deterministic forecast and the ensemble spread as the basis for a forecast uncertainty estimate," and OpenSnow's multi-model range graphs apply it directly: "if all models show a similar forecast, there is higher confidence in the forecast, and vice versa" ([Penn State EPS](https://www.e-education.psu.edu/worldofweather/s12.html); [OpenSnow multi-model graphs](https://support.opensnow.com/feature-guides/multi-model-forecast-range-graphs)). Utah practitioners favor the "NAEFS-Downscaled" plume tool, which combines the GEFS and Canadian ensemble (42 members in the 2016 description, before GEFS grew to 31) and shows 7-day total liquid and snow with each line a member and bold lines the model means ([Jans blog](https://blog.jans.com/winter-weather-basics-tools-for-planning/); [WMO NAEFS description](https://severeweather.wmo.int/TCFW/RAIV_Workshop2016/12_EnsemblePredictionSystems_EricBlake.pdf)). The public probabilistic products to read are WPC's PWPF (>=4/8/12 inches) and percentiles, WSSI-P, NBM's 10th/50th/90th percentiles and Prob>6" fields, and ECMWF's EFI and snowfall-anomaly charts. A practical threshold from the notes: if the 90th-percentile snow at a point is more than about three times the 10th, the storm is track- or ratio-sensitive and a headline number is unreliable; if the GEFS, ENS and NAEFS medians sit near one another and the deterministic runs agree, confidence is high. Two cautions apply. Members can cluster away from reality, as in the January 2015 blizzard where "spread indicated high uncertainty for short-range predictions of heavy snowfall on the western flank" ([MetEd EPS guide](https://www.meted.ucar.edu/nwp/eps_products/print_6.php)); and two sites can show different plumes for the same GEFS run because each applies its own ratio, so compare like-for-like (10:1 against 10:1, Kuchera against Kuchera) ([Pivotal snowfall guide](https://home.pivotalweather.com/guides/snowfall)).

## API reference: free, browser-callable feeds and their gotchas

The research sandbox could not reach any of these hosts directly, so **every example URL below is composed from official documentation and is untested**, and live CORS headers were not inspected; the notes are explicit that the per-model key suffixes, exact JSON shapes and a few parameter enums need confirmation against a live response. Test coordinates used throughout are Alta (40.588, -111.638) and Loveland Pass / A-Basin (39.642, -105.872).

**Open-Meteo forecast API** is the only source documented as key-free, CORS-enabled and multi-model: "No API key required, CORS supported, no ads, no tracking, not even cookies," under CC-BY 4.0 with attribution required, free for non-commercial use under 10,000 calls per day, 5,000 per hour and 600 per minute, counted per IP (so a public dashboard calling from each visitor's browser spreads load as intended, while a server proxy concentrates it) ([open-meteo README](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo); [Open-Meteo Terms](https://open-meteo.com/en/terms); [GitHub discussion #853](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/discussions/853)). The endpoint is `https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast`; relevant `models=` identifiers as of October 2026 are `ncep_gfs_seamless`, `ncep_hrrr_conus`, `ncep_nbm_conus`, `ncep_nam_conus`, `ecmwf_ifs025`, `ecmwf_aifs025_single`, `dwd_icon_seamless`, `cmc_gem_seamless` (and `cmc_gem_hrdps`), `ukmo_seamless` and `best_match`, with the backend still accepting legacy un-prefixed names, so use prefixed names with a fallback ([website options.ts](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/options.ts); [ForecastapiController.swift](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo/blob/main/Sources/App/Controllers/ForecastapiController.swift)). Model specs on that endpoint: GFS 0.11° hourly to 16 days every 6 h, HRRR 3 km hourly to 18 h (48 h at 00/06/12/18Z), NBM 2.5 km hourly to 36 h then 3-hourly to 11 days updated hourly, NAM 3 km to 60 h ([gfs-api page](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/gfs-api/+page.svelte)). The snow variables: hourly `snowfall` in cm ("For the water equivalent in millimeter, divide by 7"), `snow_depth` in meters, `freezing_level_height` in meters above sea level, `precipitation_probability` (ensemble-derived; NBM native), daily `snowfall_sum` and `snowfall_water_equivalent_sum`, and pressure-level variables on the pattern `{variable}_{level}hPa` for `temperature`, `relative_humidity`, `wind_speed`, `wind_direction`, `vertical_velocity` and `geopotential_height` at levels including 850, 700, 600 and 500 ([docs page](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/main/src/routes/en/docs/+page.svelte)). The critical gotcha: for GFS, HRRR, ECMWF, ICON and GEM, "Snowfall is derived by converting it at 0.7 cm per mm," a fixed 7:1 ratio; only "NBM statistical blend additionally provides native snowfall, snowfall height and precipitation-type probabilities" ([gfs-api page](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/gfs-api/+page.svelte)). Divide `snowfall` by 0.7 to recover liquid mm (or use `snowfall_water_equivalent_sum` directly) and apply a ratio from Check 5. Useful parameters: `elevation=` (statistical downscaling target; `nan` disables it), `cell_selection=land|nearest`, `forecast_days` up to 16, `past_days` up to 92, `temperature_unit=fahrenheit`, `precipitation_unit=inch`, and `timezone=America/Denver`, which daily variables require ([api-parameters.svelte](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/lib/components/docs/api-parameters.svelte)). A composed Alta request pulling the fields a skier needs for Checks 2-5:

`https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast?latitude=40.588&longitude=-111.638&hourly=snowfall,precipitation,snow_depth,temperature_2m,freezing_level_height,precipitation_probability,temperature_700hPa,relative_humidity_700hPa,wind_speed_700hPa,wind_direction_700hPa,wind_speed_850hPa&daily=snowfall_sum,snowfall_water_equivalent_sum,precipitation_sum,precipitation_probability_max&models=ncep_gfs_seamless,ncep_hrrr_conus,ncep_nbm_conus,ecmwf_ifs025,ecmwf_aifs025_single,dwd_icon_seamless,cmc_gem_seamless,ukmo_seamless&forecast_days=7&timezone=America%2FDenver`

and for Loveland Pass with an explicit elevation so downscaling uses the pass height: `https://api.open-meteo.com/v1/forecast?latitude=39.642&longitude=-105.872&elevation=3655&hourly=snowfall,snow_depth,temperature_2m,freezing_level_height,precipitation_probability&daily=snowfall_sum,precipitation_sum&models=ncep_nbm_conus,ncep_gfs_seamless,ecmwf_ifs025&temperature_unit=fahrenheit&precipitation_unit=inch&forecast_days=7&timezone=America%2FDenver`. Multi-model responses suffix keys per model (e.g. `snowfall_ncep_gfs_seamless`), a convention the notes could not re-verify.

**Open-Meteo Ensemble API** at `https://ensemble-api.open-meteo.com/v1/ensemble` returns per-member hourly series (interpolated to 1-hour steps) for GEFS (`ncep_gefs025`, 31 members, 0.25° to 10 days; `ncep_gefs05` to 35 days), ECMWF IFS ENS (`ecmwf_ifs025_ensemble`, 51 members, 15 days, 00z/06z runs only with a reduced variable set), AIFS ENS (`ecmwf_aifs025_ensemble`, 51), Canadian GEPS (21 members, 16 days, 39 on Mon/Thu), MOGREPS-G (18), AIGEFS (31) and Google WeatherNext 2 (64), with `snowfall`, `snow_depth`, `snowfall_water_equivalent`, `freezing_level_height` and the same pressure-level set ([ensemble-api page](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/ensemble-api/+page.svelte); [ensemble-options.ts](https://github.com/open-meteo/open-meteo-website/blob/main/src/routes/en/docs/ensemble-options.ts)). Composed example: `https://ensemble-api.open-meteo.com/v1/ensemble?latitude=40.588&longitude=-111.638&hourly=snowfall,precipitation,temperature_2m,freezing_level_height&daily=snowfall_sum&models=ncep_gefs025,ecmwf_ifs025_ensemble&forecast_days=7&timezone=America%2FDenver`. Each call returns 31-51 member series per variable, so keep variable lists short and cache per 6-hour run; member keys are widely documented as `temperature_2m_member01`... with the control unsuffixed, but that is recollection to verify.

**NWS API (api.weather.gov)** is free and CORS-enabled, with one trap: do not set a custom `User-Agent` from browser JavaScript, because it triggers a failing preflight; an NWS maintainer confirmed "Using the default UA in a client-side application is fine" and the uniqueness suggestion "is for server-side applications (or proxies)" ([weather-gov/api discussion #312](https://github.com/weather-gov/api/discussions/312)). Call `https://api.weather.gov/points/40.588,-111.638` to get `gridId` (expect `SLC`), `gridX`, `gridY` and the forecast URLs; for Loveland Pass, `/points/39.642,-105.872` should return `BOU` but sits near the BOU/GJT/PUB boundary, so trust the response. Snowfall is absent from the structured `/forecast` endpoint (only in `detailedForecast` text); it lives in the raw grid at `https://api.weather.gov/gridpoints/{gridId}/{gridX},{gridY}` as `properties.snowfallAmount.values[]`, with `validTime` encoded as ISO-8601 intervals like `2020-10-22T12:00:00+00:00/PT6H`, values that are per-period rather than cumulative, and a unit of `wmoUnit:mm` that is **snow depth in millimeters, not liquid** (an example value of 208.28 mm converts to exactly 8.2 inches), so divide by 25.4, split or weight by each period's duration, then sum into days ([weather-gov/api discussion #701](https://github.com/weather-gov/api/discussions/701); [discussion #266](https://github.com/weather-gov/api/discussions/266)). The same grid carries `quantitativePrecipitation` in liquid mm, `probabilityOfPrecipitation`, `snowLevel`, `temperature` and `windSpeed`; `snowfallAmount / quantitativePrecipitation` gives the forecaster's implied ratio per period. AFD text is retrievable at `https://api.weather.gov/products/types/AFD/locations/SLC` (also `BOU`, `GJT`, `PUB`), then `@graph[0]['@id']` to `/products/{id}` for `productText` ([NWS product types](https://forecast.weather.gov/product_types.php)). Open issues worth knowing include "How can I reliably get precipitation amounts from the NWS API?" (September 2026, unanswered) and intermittent 500/503s, so retry with backoff ([weather-gov/api discussions](https://github.com/weather-gov/api/discussions)).

**NRCS AWDB REST API (SNOTEL)** at `https://wcc.sc.egov.usda.gov/awdbRestApi/services/v1/` (Swagger at `/awdbRestApi/swagger-ui/index.html`) returns `WTEQ` (snow water equivalent, inches), `SNWD` (snow depth, inches), `PREC` and `TOBS` by station triplet in `nnn:ST:SNTL` form with `duration=DAILY|HOURLY`; composed examples are `.../v1/stations?stationTriplets=766:UT:SNTL&activeOnly=true` and `.../v1/data?stationTriplets=766:UT:SNTL&elements=WTEQ,SNWD,PREC,TOBS&duration=DAILY&beginDate=2026-10-06&endDate=2026-10-13` ([kbvernon/awdb README](https://github.com/kbvernon/awdb); [snotel-mcp-server](https://mcpservers.org/servers/jymmyt/snotel-mcp-server)). The legacy Report Generator exposes the same data as CSV at a stable pattern, e.g. `https://wcc.sc.egov.usda.gov/reportGenerator/view_csv/customSingleStationReport/daily/538:co:SNTL%7Cid=%22%22%7Cname/-7,0/WTEQ::value,WTEQ::delta,SNWD::value,SNWD::delta` for the last seven days ([NRCS report generator example](https://wcc.sc.egov.usda.gov/reportGenerator/view_csv/customSingleStationReport/daily/538:co:SNTL%7Cid=%22%22%7Cname/-7,0/WTEQ::value,WTEQ::delta,SNWD::value,SNWD::delta)). CORS for this host is unverified in either direction, `SNWD` is a sonic sensor that shows noise and negatives in shallow snow, and the station triplets nearest each resort should be confirmed via `/stations` before hard-coding.

**WPC, NOHRSC and avalanche feeds.** WSSI and WSSI-P are NOAA ArcGIS vector services at `https://mapservices.weather.noaa.gov/vector/rest/services/outlooks/wpc_wssi/MapServer` and `.../wpc_wssi_p/MapServer`, queryable as GeoJSON (standard ArcGIS syntax such as `query?geometry=-111.638,40.588&geometryType=esriGeometryPoint&inSR=4326&outFields=*&f=geojson`), with WSSI-P covering hours 24-168 only and static images updating at 01, 09, 13, 19 and 21 UTC ([wpc_wssi_p MapServer](https://mapservices.weather.noaa.gov/vector/rest/services/outlooks/wpc_wssi_p/MapServer); [WPC WSSI page](https://www.wpc.ncep.noaa.gov/wwd/wssi/wssi_expr.php)). NOHRSC's SNODAS SWE and depth rasters are an ArcGIS raster service updated daily with no official point JSON endpoint ([NOHRSC_Snow_Analysis MapServer](https://mapservices.weather.noaa.gov/raster/rest/services/snow/NOHRSC_Snow_Analysis/MapServer/?f=pjson)). The Utah Avalanche Center publishes `utahavalanchecenter.org/forecast/{region}/json` for salt-lake, provo, logan, ogden, uintas, skyline, moab, abajos and southwest, issued daily between 5 and 8 AM, but blocks "off-the-shelf" user agents ([UAC forecast API](https://utahavalanchecenter.org/docs/api/forecast)); the undocumented but stable `https://api.avalanche.org/v2/public/products/map-layer/{CENTER}` and `.../product?type=forecast&center_id=...&zone_id=...` serve CAIC and UAC zone GeoJSON and forecast JSON (bottom line, hazard and weather discussion, danger by elevation) with no authentication ([svenjr/special-barnacle](https://github.com/svenjr/special-barnacle)); CAIC's own `api.avalanche.state.co.us/api/v2/...` endpoints are used by open-source clients ([caicpy](https://github.com/gormaniac/caicpy)). Resort-report APIs are closed: OpenSnow's API "is only available to commercial partners" with server-side keys and mandatory "Powered by OpenSnow" branding, OnTheSnow's partner API is fee-based with `x-api-key` auth and centimeter/Celsius units, and Snow-Forecast.com has no API ([OpenSnow API](https://opensnow.com/api/index.html); [Mountain News Partner API](https://partner.docs.onthesnow.com)). Synoptic (MesoWest) station data now limits no-cost "Open Access" to .edu users with one concurrent request, and MADIS is account-only and server-side ([Synoptic Open Access](https://synopticdata.com/open-access); [MADIS portal](https://madis-data.ncep.noaa.gov/index.html)). Raw GRIB2 from NOMADS and the NBM on AWS (`noaa-nbm-pds`) are available but impractical in a browser; Open-Meteo is effectively the free JSON wrapper over them ([NOMADS grib filter](https://nomads.ncep.noaa.gov/cgi-bin/filter_gfs_0p25.pl); [NOAA NBM on AWS](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-nbm-parallel/)). Terms of use and image URL patterns for hot-linking Pivotal Weather or Tropical Tidbits graphics could not be verified, so prefer public-domain NOAA imagery for embedding.

## A super El Niño tilts the season south while Rachel opens it

As of the October 8, 2026 CPC diagnostic discussion an **El Niño Advisory is in effect, with Niño-3.4 at +2.1°C** (Niño-3 +3.0°C, Niño-1+2 +3.9°C) and greater than 83% odds of a strong-to-very-strong event lasting into January-March 2027; secondary write-ups cite 83% odds of a three-month RONI value of at least +2.5°C for October-December, which would be the strongest since 1950 ([CPC ENSO Diagnostic Discussion](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/ensodisc.shtml); [Coyote Gulch repost](https://coyotegulch.blog/2026/10/09/the-latest-el-nino-southern-oscillation-diagnostic-discussion-october-8-2026-is-hot-off-the-presses-from-the-climate-prediction-center/); [Wittasek Substack](https://nathanbwittasek.substack.com/p/el-nino-is-not-what-moves-your-hill)). NOAA's flagship Winter Outlook is scheduled for October 15, 2026, two days after the research date, so the operative CPC guidance is the September 17 update, which favors above-normal precipitation "extending into portions of Colorado, particularly the southern part," leaves central and northern Colorado near equal chances, places northern Utah near an area where below-normal precipitation is favored with southern Utah leaning wet, and tilts temperatures warm or equal chances depending on the outlet ([Unofficial Networks Sept 17](https://unofficialnetworks.com/2026/09/17/noaa-cpc-updated-winter-outlook/); [CPR Sept 17](https://www.cpr.org/2026/09/17/colorado-2026-winter-snow-forecast/); [TownLift Aug 2026](https://townlift.com/2026/08/strong-el-nino-could-tilt-utahs-winter-warmer-and-drier/); [Climate Impact Company](https://climateimpactcompany.com/u-s-winter-2026-27-outlook-stormy-el-nino-winter-west-and-south-mild-lack-of-snow-across-north-2-2/)).

The private forecasters agree on the geography and disagree on Utah. OpenSnow's Colorado forecast says "Northern and Central Colorado ski areas west of the Divide, including Keystone, Breck, Copper, Vail, Aspen, Crested Butte, and Steamboat, do not have a clear signal in either direction," favors above-normal snow for southern and eastern Colorado with November-December and March-April the favored months, and gave Colorado "50-50" odds of an above-average winter but strong odds of a snowy spring; its mechanism is that southerly-tracking storms "induce easterly surface winds that load the east side of the Divide" and bury the San Juans, while Monarch, Crested Butte and Aspen Snowmass benefit from Four Corners tracks ([OpenSnow Colorado Winter Forecast](https://opensnow.com/news/post/2026-2027-colorado-winter-forecast); [Steamboat Pilot](https://www.steamboatpilot.com/news/opensnow-forecasters-el-nino-snowy-winter/); [OpenSnow 2026-27 preview](https://opensnow.com/news/post/2026-2027-winter-forecast-preview)). Chris Tomer's August 24 numbers are "Tahoe and the Sierra plus 30 percent, Brian Head plus 20, Snowbowl plus 20, the southern Colorado mountains plus 20, New Mexico plus 20. Wasatch normal, Aspen normal, Vail normal, Loveland normal" ([Unofficial Networks Tomer outlook](https://unofficialnetworks.com/2026/08/24/meteorologist-chris-tomer-issues-his-2026-2027-winter-forecast/)). For Utah, Ski Utah writes that "Southern Utah tends to run above average for snowfall in El Niño winters" while strong events deliver "Northern Utah snowpack that's average to a little below average, especially early in the season," and the analog record is genuinely split: 1982-83 gave the Cottonwoods 800-900 inches and 1997-98 ran above normal, but 2015-16 was "a below-normal snowfall year"; OpenSnow nonetheless favors above-normal Utah snowfall and "heavily favors Park City, the Cottonwood Canyons, Snowbasin, and Southern Utah," with Salt Lake City itself below average ([Ski Utah El Niño](https://www.skiutah.com/blog/authors/yeti/elnino26-27/); [KUTV El Niño history](https://kutv.com/news/local/what-utahs-history-reveals-about-snowfall-during-strong-el-nino-winters); [Powder.com OpenSnow preview](https://www.powder.com/news/opensnow-2026-27-el-nino-preview-winter-forecast)). The shared caveat is warmth: "Strong El Niño winters tend to be warmer than normal, which could mean occasional high snow levels with storms" ([OpenSnow preview](https://opensnow.com/news/post/2026-2027-winter-forecast-preview)), which feeds directly into Check 4 and Check 5 above, because a warm southern-track storm delivers dense 8-10:1 snow at Wolf Creek and rain at low bases.

The season starts from a deficit. Denver's September 2026 averaged 69.2°F, fourth-warmest on record and the "sixth snowless September in a row"; water year 2026 was record-warm with statewide snowpack at 20% of median on May 1, 2026, about 38.5% of Colorado in extreme or exceptional drought in late September, and an aggregator figure (unofficial, verify with NRCS) of 26% of normal snowpack on October 6 ([Coyote Gulch/Denver Post Oct 6](https://coyotegulch.blog/2026/10/06/denver-just-saw-its-fourth-warmest-september-on-record-sixth-in-a-row-without-snow-the-denver-post-snowpack/); [Colorado Sun Sept 28](https://coloradosun.com/2026/09/28/colorado-says-goodbye-to-a-doozy-of-a-water-year-with-more-moisture-in-the-forecast/); [Coyote Gulch May 2026](https://coyotegulch.blog/2026/05/10/colorado-water-supply-forecasts-remain-well-below-median-following-early-melt-a-record-warm-and-dry-winter-in-colorado-has-resulted-in-one-of-the-most-anomalous-water-years-in-the-snotel-observing-p/)). Last season was dismal: no Colorado area had measured 70 inches by December 26, 2025, and most finished under 60% of average (Loveland 48%, Copper 62%) ([Colorado Politics](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2025/12/26/colorado-ski-areas-suffer-nobody-has-measured-over-70-inches-of-total-snowfall-for-winter-2025-26/); [bestsnow.net seas26](https://bestsnow.net/seas26.htm)). ### October 13-20, 2026: tropical moisture, a Great Basin trough, and a textbook southwest-flow split

The week's storm is a worked example of the five checks, and the notes date every claim because none of the October 13 AFDs could be read directly. The moisture source is explicitly tropical: the "remnants of former-Hurricane Rachel" brought heavy rain, flash flooding and a Big Cottonwood Canyon closure over October 10-12, with most Utah mountain sites over an inch of rain and the tops of the Cottonwoods and Timpanogos "closer to 5 inches of precipitation"; Steenburgh placed the heaviest totals "on the Alpine Ridge above Little Cottonwood Canyon" ([OpenSnow Utah post 44612](https://opensnow.com/dailysnow/utah/post/44612); [KSL](https://www.ksl.com/article/51635388/tropical-storm-remnants-forecast-to-produce-utahs-first-big-mountain-snow-of-the-season); [Wasatch Weather Weenies](https://wasatchweatherweenies.blogspot.com/)). No source called this an atmospheric river. The cold part arrives as "a potent mid-level trough digs eastward from the Great Basin and crosses Colorado from late Tuesday through Thursday" ([BoulderCAST Oct 12](https://bouldercast.com/this-week-in-colorado-weather-october-12-2026/)). ## Conclusion

The single most useful shift for a skier is to stop reading snow maps as answers and start reading them as the product of three separable inputs, liquid, ratio and terrain, each of which the models get wrong in a known direction. Liquid is low at the crests by a quarter to a third in every model verified against Alta and Colorado SNOTEL sites; the ratio applied by most free maps (10:1, 7:1 in Open-Meteo) is below the 13-18:1 these ranges typically produce in cold northwest flow; and the terrain term is invisible in any grid coarser than the HRRR. The human sources that rank highest in this report earn their place precisely because they correct those three errors in words, and the free AFD plus the free OpenSnow text already contain most of that correction. The highest-value automation for a dashboard is therefore not another model but a per-mountain overlay that reads 700-mb wind direction and crest temperature from Open-Meteo's pressure-level fields, maps them against the flow table above, and flags when the ensemble's 90th percentile exceeds three times its 10th.

Two things will change the picture within weeks. The RRFS/REFS cutover on October 14 and a possible GFS v17 implementation will rename or break products across third-party sites, and the October 15 NOAA Winter Outlook will replace the September 17 guidance; both should be re-checked before the ski-area table and seasonal section are republished. The table itself is the weakest part of the evidence base: roughly half its coordinates, drive times and SNOTEL IDs are unverified general knowledge, and no measured direction-by-resort climatology exists for any Colorado area, so the "who wins" rules remain forecaster consensus that a season of SNOTEL liquid against Open-Meteo 700-mb wind could, for the first time, actually test.
